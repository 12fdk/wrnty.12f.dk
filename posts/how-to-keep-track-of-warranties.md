You almost certainly own more warranties than you could name right now. The phone in your hand, the laptop on your desk, the washing machine, the headphones, the bike, the sofa — most of them came with a guarantee, and most of them will quietly expire without you ever thinking about it again.

That is where the money leaks out. Products rarely fail at a convenient moment; they fail a few weeks after the cover ran out, and you find yourself paying for a repair you were entitled to have for free. The problem is almost never that people throw away their rights on purpose. It is that warranties are invisible: there is no date in your calendar, no reminder, and the receipt is somewhere in a drawer, an inbox, or a landfill.

The fix is not a better filing cabinet. It is a small, repeatable system you can set up in an afternoon and then barely think about again.

## Capture the proof at the moment of purchase

The single highest-value habit is to record a warranty the day you buy the thing, not later. Later never comes, and thermal-paper receipts fade to blank within a year or two — often before the warranty they prove has even expired.

So at the point of purchase, capture three things:

- **The receipt or order confirmation.** A photo of a paper receipt is fine; forward an email receipt to yourself so it is easy to find.
- **The serial number.** Photograph the serial plate on the device or appliance. Many manufacturer claims need it, and it is a nuisance to find once the item is installed or wall-mounted.
- **The item itself.** One quick photo so you can tell three near-identical black chargers apart in two years.

Two minutes, once, at the moment you already have the receipt in your hand.

## Record the four facts that actually matter

You do not need to catalogue everything about a purchase. For a warranty, four facts do the job:

1. **What it is** — make, model, and where you bought it.
2. **When you bought it** — the purchase date.
3. **How long the warranty runs** — so you can work out the expiry date.
4. **What it cost** — useful for insurance, and for deciding whether a repair is even worth claiming.

Write those down somewhere consistent. A spreadsheet works. A note works. What matters is that all of them live in one place, in the same format, so you are never hunting across three apps to answer one question.

## Set a reminder, not a filing system

Here is the part almost everyone gets wrong: they organise the paperwork and then still miss the claim. Organising is not the goal — *acting before the deadline* is. A perfectly filed receipt for a warranty that expired last month is worth nothing.

So for every item, the important output is a reminder a little **before** the expiry date — enough lead time to actually book the repair or start the claim while the cover still stands. If something has been playing up, that nudge is your cue to get it looked at now rather than a fortnight too late.

This is the whole reason [wrnty](/) exists: you record the purchase once, and it works out the expiry date and reminds you in good time, on your Home and Lock Screen, so the deadline comes to you instead of slipping past.

## Keep it all in one searchable place

The final piece is retrieval. When something breaks, you want the receipt, the serial number and the dates in one tap — not a ten-minute dig through a drawer or a search across your email. Whatever tool you use, make sure you can answer "when does this expire and where's the proof?" instantly, because that is the only moment the whole system has to pay off.

If you buy protection plans and extended warranties too, the same system applies — though whether those plans are worth buying in the first place is a separate question, and one worth answering before you sign up. We dug into it in [is an extended warranty worth it?](/blog/is-an-extended-warranty-worth-it/).

## Start small, today

You do not need to catalogue years of past purchases this weekend. Start with the expensive things you still own that are plausibly under warranty — the phone, the laptop, the big appliances — and capture those. Then make the two-minute habit stick for everything new. Within a couple of months you will have a complete, current picture of what you own, what it is worth, and exactly when each guarantee runs out. That is the difference between hoping you kept the receipt and knowing you did.
