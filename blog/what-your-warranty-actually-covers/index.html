What a warranty actually covers on phones, laptops and gadgets

Your new phone or laptop came with a warranty. But that warranty does not cover everything — and knowing what it leaves out could save you a lot of money.

Your phone, laptop, or tablet arrived with a warranty. You probably did not read it, and that is normal — no one reads the warranty when they are excited about a new gadget.

But here is the thing: a warranty does not cover everything that goes wrong with your device. Most people assume "warranty" means "if it breaks, they fix it." It does not mean that. It means something much narrower, and the distinction matters a lot when something goes wrong.

What a standard warranty actually covers

A standard manufacturer warranty — the one that comes free with your phone, laptop, or tablet — covers defects in materials and workmanship. That is the legal phrase. In plain English, it means:

If something goes wrong through normal use without external damage, the manufacturer will repair or replace it.

This covers things like:

  • A laptop screen that develops dead pixels or lines without any impact
  • A phone that randomly restarts or shuts down
  • A camera body whose autofocus mechanism fails
  • A tablet whose home button stops responding

The key word is without external damage. If the device broke because you dropped it, spilled liquid on it, or subjected it to impact or heat beyond normal conditions, that is not a manufacturing defect. That is damage.

Rule of thumb: a warranty is a promise about the quality of the product when it leaves the factory. It is not an insurance policy for the life of the device.

What a standard warranty does NOT cover

This is the part that surprises people. The things almost never covered by a standard warranty include:

Dropped or cracked screens. Every manufacturer warranty on phones and laptops excludes damage from drops, impacts, and cracked screens. If your phone's screen is shattered, that is not a defect.

Water or liquid damage. Even a splash can void the warranty — some brands have liquid-indicator strips that show whether moisture entered the device. One dip in that strip and the warranty claim is rejected.

Battery wear and degradation. Batteries are consumables. They wear down. A warranty will not replace a battery that has degraded from normal use, even if the battery has not yet fully failed. This is one of the most common complaints people have, and manufacturers are consistent about it.

Theft and loss. A warranty covers the device you own. It does not cover the device you lost. Theft or misplacement is an insurance matter, not a warranty matter.

Cosmetic damage. Scratches, dents, or wear to the body are not covered.

Software modifications. On some devices, jailbreaking, rooting, or flashing custom firmware voids the warranty — at least for any damage that modification could have caused.

And of course, anything that happens after the warranty expires is not covered. The standard warranty on phones and laptops is typically one year. On laptops, some business models go up to three years.

Extended warranties and accidental-damage plans

This is where things get complicated, because the term "extended warranty" means different things to different people.

The manufacturer's extended warranty. Some brands offer to extend the standard warranty for an additional period — usually one or two extra years. This covers the same defects but gives you more time. Think of it like buying longer insurance against manufacturing defects.

Accidental-damage protection. This is a different product entirely. AppleCare+, Samsung Care+, their equivalents — these cover drops, spills, and broken screens. They extend the duration and they extend the scope, but they cost significantly more.

Here is a practical way to think about it:

  • If you are careful with your devices, you probably do not need accidental-damage protection. Your standard warranty already covers the things that most devices actually fail at — the defects.
  • If you are not careful — you drop your phone, you carry your laptop in an unlined bag, you keep drinks on the same desk — then accidental-damage cover might save you money.
  • If the device is expensive and the extended plan costs a lot relative to the device price, the maths may not work out even if you are careless. Compare the plan's price plus any per-incident deductible against the cost of a replacement.

There is no universal answer. It depends on your habits, the device's price, and the terms of the plan. But knowing what the standard warranty already covers is the first step — because a lot of people think they need extended cover for things the standard plan already handles.

The fine print you should actually read

Not the whole thing — but two sections, in order.

The coverage section. This tells you what the manufacturer will do if something goes wrong. Read this after you have used the device for a few days and noticed something unusual. It will tell you whether your issue falls within their scope.

The exclusion section. This is arguably more useful — it lists what they will not do. Drops, liquids, opened devices, third-party repairs. Once you know the exclusions, you can avoid the things that void your cover. Many warranties are voided not by the fault itself but by something the user did afterward — opening the device to try a repair, or using an unauthorized service.

What if a warranty claim gets refused?

This happens more often than most people expect. A claim gets turned down because:

  • The liquid indicator was triggered
  • The device shows signs of impact damage
  • The serial number does not match any purchase record
  • The device was opened or modified
  • The warranty had already expired

If a claim is refused, you have three options:

  1. Ask for an explanation and a copy of the inspection report. Some manufacturers will provide details of why the claim was rejected.
  2. Check your statutory rights. A manufacturer warranty is voluntary. In many countries, you have a separate statutory right against the seller that the product will be of satisfactory quality — and that right may still apply even after the manufacturer warranty expires. This is the subject of our guide on how long warranties last on common items.
  3. Try a different route. If you paid by credit card, some chargeback schemes or purchase-protection add-ons cover things the manufacturer warranty does not.

Our guide on making a warranty claim walks through the process step by step — from the first call to what to do if they say no.

The practical takeaway

Here is what you should walk away with:

  1. Your standard warranty covers defects, not accidents. Cracks, spills, and battery wear are not included.
  2. Know when it expires. The fastest way to find out whether anything you own is still under cover is to check your records. If you do not have records, it is not too late to start. That is what wrnty does — it keeps a running list of your products, purchase dates, and warranty expiry dates, so you always know what is still under cover. A spreadsheet or a folder in your email inbox works too, but a dedicated tracker means the dates come to you instead of the other way around.
  3. Decide about extended cover based on your habits, not fear. Not every gadget needs an extended plan.
  4. Keep your proof of purchase. Without a receipt, even a valid claim can be turned down.

When something breaks, you want to know within minutes whether your device is under cover or not. The difference between "oh good, they will fix it" and "that is going to cost me" comes down to two facts — the purchase date and the warranty length. Recording those two things takes thirty seconds, and it is the difference between surprise and certainty.

FAQ

This section covers the most common questions people ask about what a warranty covers and how to tell the difference between the standard plan and the add-ons. If you need more detail, the posts linked above walk through specific scenarios.

Common questions

What does a standard warranty actually cover?

A standard manufacturer warranty covers defects in materials and workmanship — things that go wrong through normal, careful use without any external damage. If your phone's screen goes blank for no reason, or a laptop's keyboard stops working, that is usually covered. Cracked screens, water damage, dropped devices, battery wear, and theft are almost never included.

What does a warranty NOT cover?

Standard warranties almost always exclude damage from accidents — drops, cracked screens, spills, water damage. Battery wear and degradation is also excluded because manufacturers consider batteries consumables. Theft, loss, and cosmetic damage are not covered. The fine print also usually voids a warranty if you opened the device or ran a software modification that caused the fault.

What is the difference between a warranty and an extended warranty?

A standard warranty is included with your purchase and covers manufacturing defects for a set period — typically one year for phones and laptops. An extended warranty costs extra and usually adds accidental-damage cover, longer duration, or both. It is a separate product, and the terms vary widely between providers.

Is AppleCare or Samsung Care worth it?

Whether an accidental-damage plan is worth it depends on your habits and the device price. These plans extend the warranty and cover drops and spills, but they come with a price and often a deductible per incident. If you are careful with your devices and the extended plan is more than a third of the device's price, your standard warranty plus good habits may be enough.

How do I know if my warranty is still active?

Check your proof of purchase — the warranty almost always starts on the date shown on the receipt or order confirmation. Manufacturers can often look up your device by serial number to confirm the purchase date. If you do not have the receipt, some brands use the serial number to estimate the date, but others simply will not honour the warranty without proof.

What should I do before the warranty expires?

Check whether anything you own is still under cover, and if so, decide whether you need it repaired or replaced now. A device that has been acting up for months might suddenly be fixed when a warranty claim is involved. If the device is still working and you do not need it fixed, there is nothing to do — but make sure you record the expiry so you know the next date to watch.
