Is an extended warranty worth it? A simple way to decide

The salesperson asks if you'd like to add protection for a few pounds a month. Here's how to answer in ten seconds, without the pressure.

You have decided on the item, you are at the till, and the question comes: "Would you like to add protection for a few pounds a month?" There is a small queue behind you, the assistant is friendly, and saying yes is the path of least resistance. It is also, most of the time, how retailers make some of their easiest money.

Extended warranties and protection plans are among the most profitable things a shop sells. A large share of what you pay is margin, and staff are often encouraged to sell them. That alone does not make them a scam — but it is a strong hint that the default answer should be no, and that a yes should be a deliberate decision based on numbers, not on the pressure of the moment.

Here is a way to make that decision quickly and confidently.

First, count the cover you already have

Most protection plans partly duplicate rights and cover you already hold. Before you pay for more, remember what is already yours:

  • Your statutory rights. In the EU you have a legal right to a remedy for goods that are faulty, typically for two years, independent of any warranty. Many countries have similar consumer-protection law. This is free and cannot be sold back to you.
  • The manufacturer warranty. Almost everything comes with one — often a year, sometimes far longer. A plan whose first year just mirrors this is adding nothing in year one.
  • Your card and home insurance. Some credit cards include purchase protection against damage or theft for a period after purchase, and home contents insurance may already cover expensive items.

Add those up and a surprising number of protection plans are covering a risk you have already paid to cover somewhere else.

Then do the ten-second maths

A protection plan is a bet. You are paying a fixed amount so the seller carries the risk of a repair. To know whether it is a fair bet, weigh three numbers:

  1. The plan price over its full term.
  2. The likely repair or replacement cost if the thing fails.
  3. The rough odds that it fails within the covered period, beyond the cover you already have.

If the plan costs a meaningful fraction of the item's price, and the item is generally reliable, the expected cost of just paying for a repair yourself is almost always lower than the plan. That is precisely why the plans are so profitable: they are priced to win for the seller across thousands of customers.

When a plan can actually make sense

The framework is not "always say no." There are real cases where a plan is worth it:

  • Expensive items with expensive repairs and a real failure rate — where one claim would genuinely hurt.
  • Accidental damage you are otherwise not covered for — a laptop or phone you carry everywhere, where you know your own habits.
  • Peace of mind you value concretely — sometimes not thinking about it is worth a few pounds to you, and that is a legitimate reason as long as you have seen the numbers.

If you do buy, read what is excluded, check whether there is an excess per claim, and confirm whether it is repair or replacement. A plan you do not understand is one you will not successfully claim on.

Whatever you decide, write it down

Here is the part people forget: an extended warranty you cannot find the paperwork for, or whose end date you have lost track of, is barely better than no warranty at all. If you buy the plan, the plan itself is now one more thing to track — with its own expiry date, its own document, and its own claim process.

That is exactly the kind of thing wrnty is built to hold: the plan document, the purchase date, the expiry, and a reminder before it lapses, sitting right next to the item it protects. The same discipline applies to every guarantee you own, which is the subject of our companion piece, how to keep track of warranties.

The bottom line

Treat every extended-warranty offer as a no until the numbers say otherwise. Count the cover you already have, weigh the price against the realistic repair cost, and refuse to decide under queue pressure — "I'll think about it" is a complete sentence. Do that, and you will keep the plans that genuinely protect you and skip the ones that only protect the retailer's margin.

Common questions

Are extended warranties ever worth buying?

Sometimes. They can make sense for expensive items with costly repairs and a genuine failure rate, or where accidental damage is likely and otherwise uncovered — a laptop you carry everywhere, for example. For most cheap or reliable items, the expected repair cost is far lower than the plan price, so they are not worth it.

What cover do I already have before buying a plan?

More than you think. You have your statutory rights against the retailer for faulty goods (two years in the EU), the manufacturer warranty, and sometimes purchase protection from your credit card or your home contents insurance. Check those first — a protection plan often duplicates cover you already paid for.

Why do shops push extended warranties so hard?

Because the margin is enormous — often a large share of the plan price is profit, and staff may be incentivised to sell them. That is not a reason they are always bad, but it is a strong reason to be sceptical and to decide on the numbers rather than on the spot.

What should I check in the terms before buying?

Look at what is excluded, whether it is repair or replacement, any excess or deductible you pay per claim, whether accidental damage is covered, and how long the cover lasts versus the manufacturer warranty you already have. A plan whose first year simply duplicates the free manufacturer warranty is worth less than it looks.
